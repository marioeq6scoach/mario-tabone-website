COMMUNICATION & INFLUENCE

Why Good Ideas Don't Always Land

You've done the research. You've thought through the problem. You know your recommendation makes sense. So why doesn't everyone else see it?

This is something I see frequently with intelligent, analytical professionals. They're often very good at understanding complex problems, but communicating those ideas to other people is an entirely different skill.

Being right isn't always enough

Imagine you're in a meeting presenting a solution to a problem. You've prepared the facts, considered the alternatives and developed what you believe is the best approach.

But instead of enthusiasm, you get polite nods, a few questions, and someone says, "Let's think about it."

You leave wondering what went wrong.

The problem may not be your idea. It may be the way the idea was received.

People don't process information the same way

Analytical thinkers often focus on logic, evidence and accuracy. Those are valuable strengths.

But other people may be listening through a different lens. They may be thinking about risk, relationships, priorities, time pressures or how your proposal affects them personally.

If you explain an idea entirely from your own perspective, you can unintentionally overlook what matters most to the person listening.

Three ways to make your ideas connect

1. Understand your audience before explaining your solution

Before presenting an idea, ask yourself: What matters most to this person? What problem are they trying to solve? What concerns might they have?

The better you understand their perspective, the easier it becomes to communicate in a way that resonates.

2. Start with the outcome, not the technical details

Analytical professionals sometimes explain how they reached a conclusion before explaining why the conclusion matters.

Try starting with the result or benefit, then provide the supporting detail when it's needed.

3. Make it a conversation, not a presentation

Instead of delivering everything you know, pause and invite the other person into the discussion.

Ask questions. Listen carefully. Notice their reactions. Check whether your explanation is making sense.

Communication becomes more effective when people feel involved rather than simply informed.

The real skill is connection

You don't need to become louder, more outgoing or less analytical to communicate effectively.

Your intelligence and expertise are already valuable. The opportunity is learning how to make those strengths more accessible, understandable and relevant to the people around you.

That's where communication, rapport and social intelligence come together.
